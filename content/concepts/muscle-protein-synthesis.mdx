## What it is

Muscle is in constant turnover. Protein is broken down and rebuilt continuously, and the size of a muscle reflects the running balance between the two. Muscle protein synthesis — MPS — is the building side of that ledger.

Training raises MPS. Eating protein raises MPS. Doing both raises it more than either alone. When elevated synthesis outpaces breakdown for long enough, across enough sessions, the muscle grows.

The specific fraction that matters for size is **myofibrillar** protein synthesis — the contractile machinery itself, rather than the mitochondrial or connective-tissue fractions that also respond to exercise.

## What the evidence says

The time course is the practically important part, and it has been measured directly.

<Claim grade="strong">

Muscle protein synthesis rose 50% at 4 hours after a heavy resistance training bout and 109% at 24 hours, then fell back to within 14% of the untrained control arm by approximately 36 hours. <Cite id="macdougall-1995-mps-timecourse" />

</Claim>

So a session buys you roughly a day and a half of elevated building, with the peak somewhere around the 24-hour mark. After that the muscle is, physiologically speaking, no longer responding to that session.

This is the argument for frequency. If you train a muscle once a week, it spends roughly 36 hours building and five and a half days at baseline. Train it twice, and you get two elevation windows from the same total weekly work.

But the argument is cleaner than the evidence.

<Claim grade="mixed">

When weekly volume was held constant between conditions, training frequency did not significantly influence hypertrophy across 25 studies. <Cite id="schoenfeld-2019-frequency" />

</Claim>

Both of those things are true, and reconciling them is the interesting part. The MPS time course says frequency should matter. The volume-equated trials say that once total weekly sets are fixed, it largely does not. The most likely explanation is that frequency is not a driver in its own right — it is a *tool for distributing volume*. Twenty sets of chest in one session is a worse experience than ten sets twice, but not because of the synthesis window; it is because the last sets of a very long session are performed fatigued, at degraded quality, and drift toward junk volume.

There is a second complication worth knowing about, because it is the reason a lot of older MPS research is now read differently.

<Claim grade="strong">

Early in a training programme, increases in myofibrillar protein synthesis correlated with muscle *damage* rather than with subsequent hypertrophy. Only after damage had attenuated, by around week 10, did protein synthesis correlate with actual muscle growth. <Cite id="damas-2016-mps-damage" />

</Claim>

In other words: an acute spike in MPS after a session is not automatically a growth signal. In an unaccustomed lifter, much of it is repair. This is why acute MPS studies — the kind that produce headlines about a supplement or a protocol "increasing muscle protein synthesis by X%" — are weak evidence for hypertrophy on their own.

## What it means in practice

**Train each muscle about twice a week.** Not because the synthesis window demands it, but because it is the most practical way to distribute a productive weekly volume across sessions short enough to perform well. Two to three times is fine; the app defaults to two.

**Do not read acute MPS results as growth results.** If a study measured protein synthesis for a few hours after a single session and did not measure muscle size over months, it is a mechanistic result. Grade it accordingly.

**Eat enough protein, spread across the day.** The app does not prescribe diets, and will never prescribe aggressive restriction — but adequate protein is the input to the process this whole concept describes.

## Common mistakes

**Treating the 48-hour window as a deadline.** Missing it does not undo anything. Frequency is a distribution tool, not a timer you are racing.

**Chasing soreness as evidence of synthesis.** Soreness tracks damage, and damage-driven MPS is largely repair. See muscle damage.

**Assuming more frequency is better without adding volume.** Splitting the same ten weekly sets across four sessions instead of two does not obviously help, and the volume-equated evidence says it does not. Frequency earns its keep by letting you do *more total quality work*, not by subdividing the same amount.

<Uncertainty>

The MPS time course comes from a small mechanistic study — six participants, one arm trained, in 1995. The finding has held up and is consistent with later work, but it is not a large literature, and the 24-to-48-hour figure is used far more confidently across fitness media than its evidence base warrants.

The tension between the mechanistic case for frequency and the volume-equated trials showing no frequency effect is unresolved. Our reading — that frequency is a volume-distribution tool — is an interpretation, not a finding. If future work shows a frequency effect independent of volume, that interpretation is wrong.

</Uncertainty>
