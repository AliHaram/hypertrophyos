## What it is

A muscle fibre does not know how much weight is on the bar. It knows how much force it is producing, and for how long.

Mechanical tension is that force, sensed at the level of the individual fibre. When a fibre contracts against resistance, the load deforms structures in and around it — the cytoskeleton, the costameres anchoring the fibre to its surrounding matrix, the protein complexes threaded through the sarcomere. Those deformations are read by mechanosensors, which convert a physical event into a chemical one. That conversion is called mechanotransduction, and it is the beginning of every hypertrophic response.

The signal converges on <Term>mTORC1</Term>, a kinase complex that acts as the cell's decision point for whether to build new contractile protein.

<Claim grade="strong">

Mechanical loading is the primary stimulus for hypertrophy, acting through mTORC1-mediated increases in muscle protein synthesis. <Cite id="wackerhage-2019-stimuli" /> The specific mechanosensor has not been definitively identified — a filamin-C–BAG3-dependent mechanism is the leading candidate — but that the stimulus is mechanical is not seriously contested.

</Claim>

The gap between those two sentences is worth sitting with. We know with confidence *that* tension drives growth. We do not yet know exactly *what molecule reads it*. Anyone who tells you the mechanism is settled at the molecular level is ahead of the literature.

## What the evidence says

The most useful thing about tension is what it demotes.

**Load is not tension.** This is the distinction that breaks most gym intuition. A heavier bar produces more force per repetition — but a lighter bar taken close to failure produces near-maximal force on the final repetitions, because as fatigue accumulates the nervous system must recruit progressively larger motor units to maintain the same output.

<Claim grade="strong">

Over 12 weeks in 49 trained men, higher-load and lower-load training produced equivalent hypertrophy when sets were taken to volitional failure. <Cite id="morton-2016-load" /> Load selection within a broad range is not what determines growth. Proximity to failure is.

</Claim>

This is why the app treats a rep range of roughly 5 to 30 as a single usable band rather than ranking loads within it. What varies across that band is joint stress, time cost, and how unpleasant the set is — not the hypertrophic outcome.

**Tension at long muscle lengths may count for more.** A muscle produces force differently depending on how stretched it is, and there is an accumulating case that the stretched position is where the growth signal is strongest.

<Claim grade="mixed">

Across 11 studies, full range of motion and partials performed in the *lengthened* portion of the range produced greater hypertrophy than partials in the shortened portion. <Cite id="kassiano-2023-rom" /> A trial in the calves found lengthened partials beat full range of motion outright. <Cite id="kassiano-2023-gastrocnemius" />

</Claim>

The grade is <EvidenceChip grade="mixed" /> rather than <EvidenceChip grade="strong" /> deliberately, and the reason is a recent result that cuts against the enthusiasm.

<Claim grade="mixed">

In a within-participant design with 30 trained participants, lengthened partials produced adaptations *similar to* — not greater than — full range of motion. <Cite id="wolf-2025-lengthened-partials" />

</Claim>

Read together, the honest summary is: training a muscle in its lengthened position is at least as good as full range of motion, sometimes better, and clearly better than training it short. That is a weaker statement than "lengthened training is superior", which is what most of the internet currently says.

## What it means in practice

Three things follow, and they are ordered by how much they change.

1. **Take sets close enough to failure that tension is high on the last repetitions.** Load is your choice; effort is not optional. See <Term>proximity to failure</Term>.
2. **Train through a full range of motion, biased toward the stretch.** Not because long-length training is proven superior, but because it is never worse and plausibly better — an asymmetric bet at no cost.
3. **Stop treating load as the scoreboard.** A rep performed with four inches shaved off the bottom is not a heavier rep. It is a shorter one.

The third point is where most people lose progress without noticing. Range of motion is the variable that silently degrades as load climbs, and because the number on the bar goes up, it registers as progress.

## Common mistakes

**Chasing the pump as though it were the signal.** Metabolite accumulation is a *consequence* of doing enough work under tension, not an independent driver of comparable size. See <Term>metabolic stress</Term>.

**Assuming heavy always means more tension.** Above roughly 85% of one-rep maximum, you accumulate fatigue and joint stress faster than you accumulate stimulus, and the total number of high-tension repetitions per set falls. Heavy work has a place — for strength, for practising the skill of the lift — but it is not a tension maximiser.

**Adding load by removing range.** The most common invisible regression in training. The app treats range of motion as a quality gate rather than a progression lever for exactly this reason — see <Term>progressive overload</Term>.

<Uncertainty>

The molecular mechanism is genuinely unsettled. We describe tension as being "sensed" and converted to a growth signal, and that framing is well supported, but the specific sensor remains a candidate rather than a conclusion.

The long-muscle-length advantage is young literature with a mixed most-recent result. If the next several trials look like Wolf and colleagues rather than Kassiano and colleagues, the correct summary becomes "range of motion matters, position within it matters less than we thought" — and the practical advice barely changes, which is a reason to trust the advice more than the mechanism.

</Uncertainty>
