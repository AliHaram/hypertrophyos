## What it is

Every set does two things. It delivers a growth stimulus to the muscle you were aiming at, and it consumes recovery — locally, systemically, and at the joints and connective tissue involved.

Only the first is the point. The second is the bill.

Stimulus-to-fatigue ratio is the framework for thinking about that trade. Two exercises can deliver comparable stimulus to a muscle at very different fatigue costs, and when they do, the cheaper one is simply better — not as a compromise, but as the correct choice.

The canonical example is rowing. A barbell row and a chest-supported machine row both load the lats and mid-back through a similar range. The barbell row additionally demands spinal erector isometric work, hip and hamstring stabilisation, and a braced position that fails before the target muscle does. That extra cost buys nothing for lat hypertrophy. It buys a great deal for someone training to be a better barbell rower.

## What the evidence says

There is no direct evidence, and that is the honest headline. No trial has compared exercises on a validated SFR metric, because no validated metric exists.

What exists is a set of well-supported components that the framework assembles.

<Claim grade="mixed">

The stimulus side is dominated by proximity to failure and tension through the range — both established. The fatigue side is dominated by systemic load, stabilisation demand, and joint stress — all real, all measurable in isolation, none of them integrated into a single validated index.

</Claim>

The strongest supporting evidence is indirect and comes from what happens when fatigue is allowed to eat into work quality.

<Claim grade="strong">

Longer rest intervals, which reduce accumulated fatigue between sets, produced greater hypertrophy than short rest — despite short rest generating more metabolic stress. <Cite id="schoenfeld-2016-rest" />

</Claim>

That is an SFR result in miniature: reducing fatigue cost, while holding stimulus roughly constant, improved the outcome.

There is also a fatigue argument embedded in the failure literature. Sets to absolute failure deliver only a trivial hypertrophy advantage over sets stopped short <Cite id="refalo-2023-proximity" /> while costing disproportionately more fatigue — particularly on compound, high-axial-load movements. The stimulus curve flattens near failure; the fatigue curve does not.

## What it means in practice

**Judge an exercise by what it delivers to the target, not by how hard it feels.** A heavy barbell squat is more taxing than a leg press. It is not obviously a better quadriceps stimulus, and it costs far more of the week's recovery.

**Put your least stable exercises where they cost least.** If you are going to do a high-fatigue compound, do it when you are fresh and when the rest of the week can absorb it.

**Use SFR to decide substitutions, not to rank lifts absolutely.** SFR is contextual. A barbell row has poor SFR for lat hypertrophy and excellent SFR for a powerlifter who needs positional strength. The app's substitution engine matches on <Term>resistance profile</Term> and muscle length rather than on exercise name, and shows the trade it is making.

**Do not confuse low fatigue with low effort.** A high-SFR machine movement still has to be taken close to failure. Comfortable positioning is the point; comfortable sets are not.

## Common mistakes

**Treating free weights as inherently superior.** They demand more stabilisation, which is a cost. Whether that cost buys you anything depends entirely on your goal. For hypertrophy specifically, it often does not.

**Treating machines as inherently inferior.** The same error in reverse, and more common among experienced lifters, where it is usually identity rather than reasoning.

**Rating SFR as though it were measured.** The app shows an SFR score from 1 to 5 for every exercise, and always shows the reasoning next to it, because a bare number would imply a precision this framework does not have.

<Uncertainty>

The whole concept is a reasoning tool rather than a finding, which is why it carries a <EvidenceChip grade="mixed" /> despite drawing on well-established components.

Its main risk is unfalsifiability. Because SFR is not measured, it can be invoked to justify almost any exercise preference after the fact — and it frequently is, across the coaching internet. The app constrains this by requiring every SFR rating to state its reasoning in terms of specific mechanical properties, so that a rating can at least be argued with.

</Uncertainty>
