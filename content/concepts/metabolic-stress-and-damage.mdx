## What it is

For most of the 2010s, hypertrophy was taught as a three-part model: mechanical tension, metabolic stress, and muscle damage. The framing was influential, it was intuitive, and it shaped a decade of programme design — drop sets for the metabolite hit, novel exercises for the damage, heavy work for the tension.

**Metabolic stress** refers to the accumulation of metabolites — lactate, hydrogen ions, inorganic phosphate — inside a working muscle when blood flow is occluded by sustained contraction. Subjectively, it is the pump and the burn. The proposed mechanisms were cell swelling acting as an anabolic signal, and metabolite-driven increases in motor unit recruitment.

**Muscle damage** refers to microscopic disruption of the fibre and its surrounding structures — Z-band streaming, membrane disturbance, the inflammatory and satellite-cell response that follows. Subjectively, it is soreness.

The model has not held up well in its strong form.

## What the evidence says

The damage case is the clearer of the two, and the evidence against it is direct.

<Claim grade="strong">

Increases in myofibrillar protein synthesis during the first week of training tracked muscle *damage* rather than eventual hypertrophy. Damage was highest at week one, lower at week three, and minimal by week ten — and only once it had attenuated did protein synthesis correlate with actual growth. <Cite id="damas-2016-mps-damage" />

</Claim>

This effectively inverts the older reading. Damage does not appear to be a growth signal that we should try to produce. It appears to be a cost that we get better at avoiding as we adapt — and growth proceeds *more* efficiently once we do. A trained lifter who is no longer getting sore is not losing stimulus; they have stopped paying a tax.

The metabolic stress case is weaker but less conclusively settled. The most useful indirect evidence comes from rest intervals, where shorter rest reliably produces more metabolite accumulation.

<Claim grade="strong">

Over 8 weeks in trained men, three-minute rest between sets produced greater strength *and* greater hypertrophy than one-minute rest. <Cite id="schoenfeld-2016-rest" />

</Claim>

If metabolic stress were an important independent driver, the short-rest condition — which generates far more of it — should have done at least as well. It did worse. The straightforward explanation is that longer rest allowed more total quality work under high tension, and that this mattered more than the metabolite accumulation short rest produced.

Similarly, if metabolites drove growth independently, high-repetition light-load training should out-perform heavy training. It does not; the two are equivalent when effort is matched. <Cite id="morton-2016-load" />

## What it means in practice

**Stop programming for the pump.** A pump is a reliable sign that you did work, in the same way that sweat is a sign you exercised. Neither is the thing that causes adaptation.

**Stop using soreness as a quality signal.** It measures unaccustomedness, not stimulus. The strongest predictor of soreness is doing something new, not doing something productive.

**Rest long enough to perform.** Roughly two to three minutes on compound movements, less on isolation work where the systemic cost is lower. Cutting rest to chase a burn trades tension for sensation.

**Do not deliberately seek damage.** Rotating exercises constantly to "shock the muscle" produces soreness and a worse learning curve on every lift, and the app's SFR ratings are built on the opposite assumption.

## Common mistakes

**"If I'm not sore, it didn't work."** The most durable piece of gym lore in this section, and the one most clearly contradicted by the evidence. Trained lifters get sore less because they are adapted, not because they are under-training.

**Treating metabolite work as useless.** It is not — high-rep, short-rest work still produces tension near failure and still grows muscle. It just does so *through* tension, not through an additional metabolic mechanism. The distinction changes why you would choose it, not whether you may.

**Reading this section as "the 2010 model was fraudulent."** It was a reasonable synthesis of what was known at the time. It has been revised by better-designed longitudinal work. That is what should happen.

<Uncertainty>

This concept is graded <EvidenceChip grade="mixed" /> overall because the negative case is stronger for damage than for metabolic stress.

For damage, there is direct longitudinal evidence that dissociates it from hypertrophy. For metabolic stress, the case is largely inferential: protocols that maximise metabolites do not out-perform protocols that do not, therefore metabolites are probably not doing independent work. That is reasonable, but it is not the same as an experiment isolating the variable — and isolating metabolite accumulation from the muscular work that generates it is genuinely hard to do.

If a well-controlled trial found an independent effect of cell swelling on hypertrophy, this concept would need rewriting. We would consider that surprising, not impossible.

</Uncertainty>
