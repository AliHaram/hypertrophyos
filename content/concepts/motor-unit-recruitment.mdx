## What it is

A motor unit is one motor neuron and every muscle fibre it controls. It is the smallest unit of force the nervous system can command — fibres within a motor unit fire together, all or nothing.

Motor units differ enormously in size. A small one might control a few dozen fibres and produce a little force for a long time without fatiguing. A large one might control a thousand, produce a great deal of force, and fatigue within seconds.

The **size principle** governs the order they are called on: as force demand rises, the nervous system recruits motor units from smallest to largest. It never skips. You cannot choose to use your largest motor units — you can only create conditions that require them.

<Claim grade="strong">

The largest, highest-threshold motor units control the fibres with the greatest hypertrophic potential, and they are recruited last. Reaching them therefore requires either a load heavy enough to demand maximal force immediately, or a lighter load carried far enough into fatigue that maximal force becomes necessary to continue.

</Claim>

## What the evidence says

This is the mechanism underneath the load-versus-effort finding. If the size principle holds, then two very different sets should produce similar growth as long as both end near failure — and they do.

<Claim grade="strong">

Higher-load and lower-load training produced equivalent hypertrophy over 12 weeks in trained men, provided sets were taken to volitional failure. <Cite id="morton-2016-load" />

</Claim>

A set of five at a heavy load recruits high-threshold units from the first repetition. A set of twenty-five at a light load recruits them only in the last handful, once the smaller units have fatigued and can no longer meet the demand. Both sets deliver high-tension repetitions to the fibres that matter. The light set simply spends more time getting there — which is a cost in time and discomfort, not in outcome.

The corollary is uncomfortable for how most people train: repetitions performed far from failure contribute very little. They occur below the recruitment threshold of the units you are trying to grow.

<Claim grade="mixed">

Training to set failure holds only a trivial advantage over stopping short of it for hypertrophy — an effect size of 0.19, with a confidence interval reaching zero. <Cite id="refalo-2023-proximity" /> Hypertrophy trends modestly upward as sets approach failure, without a clean threshold. <Cite id="robinson-2024-proximity-dose-response" />

</Claim>

Note the shape of this. It is not "failure is required" and it is not "failure does not matter". It is a gradient with a shallow slope near the top: the last few reps before failure carry most of the stimulus, and the very last one carries only slightly more than the one before it, at a much higher fatigue cost.

## What it means in practice

**Pick your load for its side effects, not its stimulus.** Within roughly 5 to 30 repetitions, the growth outcome is similar. Choose based on joint stress, how well the movement is loaded at that rep range, and how much time you have. Heavy work punishes the joints; very light work costs time and is unpleasant. Most of your work belongs in the middle because the middle minimises both costs — not because the middle is magic.

**Get close enough to failure that the last reps are genuinely hard.** Roughly 0 to 3 reps in reserve on most working sets. See <Term>RIR</Term>.

**Recognise that "close to failure" is a skill you probably do not have yet.** This is the single most consequential fact in the app, and it has its own concept — see <Term>proximity to failure</Term>.

## Common mistakes

**Believing you can target fibre types by load.** You cannot preferentially recruit fast-twitch fibres by lifting heavy — the size principle recruits in fixed order regardless. What heavy loads do is reach the high-threshold units *sooner*, not *exclusively*.

**Treating every set as equivalent.** Three sets at 5 RIR and three sets at 1 RIR are not six comparable sets. The app's <Term>junk volume</Term> detector exists because this error is invisible in a normal training log — both look like "3 × 10".

**Reading the size principle as an argument for always training heavy.** It is closer to the opposite: because effort rather than load determines whether you reach the units that matter, you are free to choose the load that costs you least.
